How a Bill Becomes Law in New York State

From an idea to a signed law: how a bill moves through the Senate, the Assembly and the Governor's desk, and how many votes it takes at each step.

63 Senators · 150 Assembly Members Majority of each house to pass Two-thirds of each to override a veto

An idea becomes a bill

Senator or Assembly Member

Any member of the Legislature can sponsor a bill, often after hearing from constituents, advocates, agencies or the Governor.

  • Staff in each house turn the idea into legal text.
  • Members usually file a matching “same-as” bill in the other house, so both can move together.
  • Other members can co-sponsor to show support.
1

Introduction & referral

Senate or Assembly · Session

The bill is formally introduced and given a number: S for a Senate bill, A for an Assembly bill.

  • It is sent to the committee that covers the topic, like Health, Education or Housing.
  • The session runs January to early June, and a bill has two years (one session) to pass.
2

Committee review

Standing committee · Public

The committee studies the bill. Some bills get public hearings, and members hear from agencies, advocates and experts.

  • The sponsor and committee may amend the bill.
  • The committee chair largely decides whether a bill is taken up. Many never are.
3

Committee vote

Committee members

If a majority of the committee votes yes, the bill is reported out. Bills that cost money also go to the Finance (Senate) or Ways and Means (Assembly) committee, and many pass through the Rules Committee before reaching the floor.

4

Floor vote in the first house

All senators or members

The bill is placed on the calendar and debated on the floor. Under the State Constitution it must sit in print for three days before a final vote, unless the Governor certifies an emergency.

  • It passes with a majority of all members elected: 32 of 63 in the Senate, 76 of 150 in the Assembly.
  • Each Senate roll call is public. Explore them on the map.
5

The other house

Second chamber

The bill repeats the process in the other house: referral, committee and a floor vote. Often the second house takes up its own member's matching bill instead.

  • Both houses must pass the same text.
  • If one house amends it, the other must agree before it moves on.
6

The Governor decides

Governor

Bills do not go to the Governor automatically. The Legislature sends them over, often in batches through the end of the year, and the clock starts at delivery.

  • 10 days to act (not counting Sundays) while the Legislature is in session.
  • 30 days after delivery if the Legislature has adjourned for the year. A bill left unsigned then dies (a “pocket veto”).
7

Signs it

The bill becomes law and is given a chapter number. Track these on the Signed view of the map.

Does nothing

While the Legislature is in session, a bill the Governor ignores for 10 days becomes law. After adjournment it dies.

Vetoes it

It returns to the house that passed it. Overriding takes two-thirds of both houses: 42 of 63 senators and 100 of 150 Assembly Members. See the Vetoed view.

It's a Chapter of the Laws

Secretary of State · Public record

A signed bill becomes a numbered “Chapter” of that year's Laws of New York. It takes effect on the date written into it, which is often months later.

8

How many votes does it take?

The Senate has 63 seats and the Assembly has 150. Pick a threshold to see what it looks like.

Two houses, one bill

The Senate and Assembly are equals: nothing becomes law unless both pass identical text. A Senate bill (S) and its Assembly twin (A) are tracked separately until one house takes up the other's version.

The budget is different

The Governor proposes the state budget each winter and the Legislature negotiates and passes it, usually by April. Budget bills move on their own track and carry most of the year's money decisions.
